--- SESSION START ---

VP: Good afternoon, Yi-Jun. Could you start by telling us about your product management journey and what you're most proud of?

CANDIDATE: Good afternoon. I've been in product management for 6 years, currently leading the B2B SaaS platform at DataFlow Inc. What I'm most proud of is the product turnaround I led last year: our analytics product had declining engagement, so I conducted 40+ customer interviews, identified the core pain points around data visualization, and redefined the product strategy. We pivoted from a reporting tool to an insights platform, which resulted in a 3x increase in weekly active users and 50% reduction in churn within 6 months.

ED: How do you prioritize features when you have multiple stakeholders pulling in different directions?

CANDIDATE: I use a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods. First, I establish clear product OKRs that tie directly to business goals. Every feature request is evaluated against those OKRs. I use RICE scoring (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) to create an initial prioritization, then validate assumptions with user research. I also maintain a stakeholder map and hold monthly prioritization sessions where we review the roadmap together. Transparency is key — I publish the prioritization framework and monthly roadmap decisions to the entire company.

VP: Tell us about a product failure and what you learned.

CANDIDATE: Early in my career, I launched a feature based entirely on internal assumptions without proper user validation. We spent 3 months building an advanced workflow automation tool, only to find that our users preferred simpler solutions. It was a $200k mistake. I learned to always validate with prototypes before committing engineering resources. Now I insist on usability testing with clickable prototypes before any feature enters development. This has saved us from building the wrong thing multiple times.

ED: How do you work with engineering teams on technical decisions?

CANDIDATE: I believe in informed collaboration. While I don't dictate technical solutions, I invest time in understanding the technical landscape. I attend architecture reviews, learn about trade-offs, and bring the customer perspective to technical discussions. I've found that engineers appreciate a PM who understands the implications of technical debt and can advocate for refactoring time based on long-term product benefits.

VP: How do you handle a situation where a key feature is behind schedule?

CANDIDATE: First, I work with the engineering lead to understand the root cause — is it scope creep, technical complexity, or estimation error? Then I look at options: can we reduce scope while maintaining core value, can we shift non-critical items to the next release, or do we need to communicate a timeline change to stakeholders? Communication is critical — I proactively update stakeholders with the situation, impact, and revised plan rather than waiting for them to ask.

ED: What tools and methodologies do you use in your day-to-day work?

CANDIDATE: I use Linear for issue tracking, Figma for wireframes and prototypes, and Notion for documentation. Process-wise, I practice outcome-driven development: instead of measuring output (features shipped), I measure outcomes (user behavior changes). I run bi-weekly sprints with the engineering team and hold regular customer feedback sessions. For analytics, I use Amplitude and Mixpanel.
